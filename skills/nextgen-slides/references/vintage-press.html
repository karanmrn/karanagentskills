THE NIGHT THE
LIGHTS WENT OUT

Lightning Fells the Grid at Half-Past Nine; Nine Millions Plunged into a Darkness of Twenty-Five Hours

By Eleanor R. Vance  •  Chief Metropolitan Correspondent

New York. — At nine twenty-seven o’clock last evening, in the airless heat of a July heatwave, the great electric heart of this city stopped. From the Bronx to the Battery, from Broadway’s blazing marquees to the tenement stoops of Bushwick, the current failed and did not return.

What began as a bolt of lightning upon a substation at Buchanan cascaded, in scarcely one hour, into the total collapse of the Consolidated Edison network — a failure engineers had thought all but impossible after the humbling darkness of 1965.

Through the long, sweltering night that followed, a city of eight millions was left to its own devices: to candlelight and canned heat, to fear and to fellowship, to looting and to unexpected grace. This is the record of those hours.

The Scene • A City on the Boil The Daily Chronicle — 2

A Heatwave, a Storm, and a Single Fatal Bolt

For nine consecutive days the mercury had refused to fall. The pavements shimmered; the rivers gave no relief; and in the crowded flats of the boroughs, families dragged mattresses onto fire escapes in search of a breath of moving air. The demand for current, driven by ten thousand humming fans and window units, pressed against Con Edison’s every reserve.

Then came the storm. A little after half-past eight, a squall line rolled down the Hudson, and at 8:37 a bolt of lightning struck two 345-kilovolt transmission lines at the Buchanan South substation, hard by the Indian Point plant. A second strike followed. Circuit breakers, meant to protect, tripped and would not reset.

For fifty desperate minutes, dispatchers in the control room watched the load shift and stagger from one line to the next, each in turn tripping under the strain. Voltage sagged; frequency wandered. At 9:27, the last tie to the outside world parted, and the island stood alone in the dark.

By the Numbers • The Reckoning The Daily Chronicle — 4

The Long Dark, Set Down in Figures

25 hrs
In Total Darkness

From the collapse at 9:27 on the evening of the thirteenth until power was fully restored at 10:39 the following night, the metropolis endured better than a full day without current — the longest such blackout in its history.

3,776
Souls Arrested

Amid the looting that swept thirty-one neighbourhoods, the police made the largest mass of arrests the city had ever recorded in a single episode. More than a thousand fires were set; five hundred and fifty officers were injured in the tumult.

1,616
Storefronts Ruined

From Bushwick to the Grand Concourse, sixteen hundred shops were damaged or emptied. The losses are reckoned near three hundred million dollars — a wound to the poorest wards from which many merchants shall not recover.

The Aftermath • A City Takes Stock The Daily Chronicle — 6

Dawn Breaks on a Chastened Metropolis

A Sweltering Vigil

When the sun rose over the East River, it lit a city both broken and bound closer together. On darkened blocks, neighbours had passed the night on stoops, sharing melting ice and battery wirelesses, directing traffic by hand at every silent signal, and shepherding the stranded down the black stairwells of a hundred towers.

Elsewhere the night had shown a crueller face. Whole commercial strips lay gutted and smoking, their gates torn from the brick, their goods carried off into the dark. By morning the fire brigades, having answered better than a thousand alarms, stood exhausted in the ash.

Questions for Con Edison

Already the demands for answer mount. How, twelve years after the lessons of 1965, could a single storm topple the whole apparatus? Why did the automatic load-shedding fail to save the core? The Governor has ordered an inquiry; the Mayor speaks of “a night of terror and of shame.”

Yet amid the recrimination there are the quieter accounts — of the midwife who delivered a child by flashlight, of the strangers who linked arms across a flooded intersection, of the whole block that sang until the streetlamps, near eleven the next night, flickered and at last held.
