What "third wave" means

Coffee treated as a craft, not a commodity.

The phrase, coined by roaster Trish Rothgeb in 2002, describes a movement that treats coffee the way sommeliers treat wine — worthy of attention to origin, variety, and process.

The bean stops being an anonymous caffeine delivery system and becomes an agricultural product with a name, a farm, and a season.

Like wine
Origin matters
A washed Ethiopian Yirgacheffe tastes of jasmine and bergamot; a Sumatran, of cedar and cocoa. Place is in the cup.
Like wine
The maker is named
Farmer, importer, and roaster are credited by name — traceability from a specific lot back to a specific hillside.
Like wine
Vintage & season
Coffee is a harvest. A bag carries its crop year, its altitude, its processing method — read like a label.

The Roast

Green beans meet heat. Over eight to twelve minutes the roaster coaxes out sugars and acids — stopping just past first crack to protect the delicate top notes.

Grind, water temperature, and time do the rest. A patient pour-over at 94°C draws out clarity the farm spent nine months building. Then, finally, you drink.

A short history

Three waves, three very different relationships with the cup.

First Wave
Coffee everywhere
1900s–1960s
  • Ground, canned, and shelf-stable
  • Convenience over character
  • Instant coffee reaches every kitchen
  • The goal: caffeine, cheaply
Second Wave
Coffee as experience
1970s–1990s
  • The café becomes a "third place"
  • Espresso, lattes, and menus
  • Darker roasts, bolder branding
  • The goal: comfort and ritual
Third Wave · Now
Coffee as craft
2000s–today
  • Single-origin, traceable lots
  • Lighter roasts, tasting notes
  • Direct trade with named farms
  • The goal: flavor and honesty
Before your next cup

Slow down.
Ask where it came from.

The third wave isn't about snobbery — it's about attention. The next time a cup surprises you, turn the bag over. Somewhere on a hillside, a farmer meant for it to taste exactly like that.
