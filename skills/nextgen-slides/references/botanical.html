The problem underfoot

Lawns are
ecological
deserts.

Turf grass is now the single largest irrigated "crop" in America — roughly 40 million acres, an area larger than the state of Georgia. It feeds almost nothing. Clipped, sprayed, and non-native, a typical lawn offers no seeds, no host plants, and no shelter for the insects that the entire food web depends on.

40M
acres of irrigated lawn in the United States — three times the acreage of any food crop.
Where to begin

Four moves toward a wilder yard

01
Plant natives
Choose species that co-evolved with local wildlife — oaks, goldenrod, milkweed, asters. They feed pollinators and the caterpillars birds rely on.
02
Leave the leaves
Fallen leaves are winter housing. Fireflies, luna moths, and native bees overwinter in the litter — rake it into beds, don't bag it.
03
Add water
A shallow dish with stones, or a small pond, becomes a magnet — for dragonflies, thirsty birds, and amphibians within a single season.
04
Ditch the pesticides
Broad-spectrum sprays kill indiscriminately. Tolerate a few chewed leaves — a healthy garden polices its own pests.

The keystone in your yard
500+
species of caterpillar supported by a single native oak tree.
A non-native ginkgo, by comparison, supports around five. Plant the oak — you are planting a food web that a nesting chickadee needs 6,000 caterpillars to raise one brood.
Begin with one square metre

The wild is patient.
It only needs an invitation.

You don't have to convert the whole yard this weekend. Let one corner grow. Plant one native. Skip one mow. The birds, the bees, and the fireflies will find it faster than you'd believe — and your backyard will be quietly, stubbornly alive.

Go outside. Let something grow.
